Science first, code second, and the habits carried over.

A biochemistry degree, a decision to teach myself web development, and fourteen projects later. Here is how that actually happened.

I finished a BSc in Biochemistry at Abia State University without a clear plan to work in a lab. What I kept from those years was not the subject matter so much as the method: form a hypothesis about why something behaves the way it does, run a controlled test, change one variable at a time, and trust the result over your first guess.

That method turned out to transfer directly into debugging. Most of the fixes across my projects came from the same process: find the actual mechanism behind a bug rather than patching the symptom. On both the ShelemJ and Amani Community Trust sites, a mobile menu was rendering badly for what looked like unrelated reasons. In both cases the real cause was the same one, a CSS property called backdrop filter quietly changing how a fixed element gets positioned. Finding that once was useful. Recognizing it a second time, immediately, is the actual skill.

I taught myself web development from there, later training formally at Digital Fortress Institute to round out what I had picked up on my own. I do not treat being self taught as something to apologize for. Most of what I know came from taking on a real project for a real person and being accountable for whether it actually worked, which is a different kind of pressure than a tutorial provides.

Right now I split my time between freelance web development and managing social media for First Experts Logistics Solutions, a freight forwarding and customs clearance company. That second role means I am not just writing code in isolation. I spend real time thinking about how a business talks to its customers, which shows up directly in how I approach client sites: what a visitor actually needs to see first, and what can wait.

I am based in Lagos, Nigeria, and open to remote, hybrid, or on site work, including relocating internationally if the right opportunity calls for it.

How I actually work
01

Find the mechanism

A bug report is a symptom, not a diagnosis. I would rather spend an extra hour finding the actual cause than ship a fix that only works by accident.

02

No fabricated proof

If a client has no real testimonial or logo to show yet, the honest version of that section is empty or replaced with something verifiable, not invented.

03

Match the tool to the job

Some projects need a real Postgres backend and payment infrastructure. Others need none of that. I would rather build the right sized thing than the impressive sounding one.

How I debug
The method, not just the outcome

This is the same process from running lab assays, applied to code. It is not a metaphor I added afterward, it is genuinely how I found the same CSS bug twice, on two different projects, in minutes the second time.

01
Observe
The mobile menu on Amani Community Trust rendered in the wrong position. Not "broken," specifically wrong.
02
Hypothesize
Something in the header was changing how a child element's fixed positioning was calculated.
03
Test
Removed properties from the header one at a time rather than guessing at a full rewrite.
04
Isolate
A backdrop filter on the header was quietly creating a new containing block for its fixed position children.
05
Fix
Moved the mobile menu to be a direct child of the body instead of the header.
06
Verify
Confirmed the menu behaved correctly across the breakpoints it had failed at before.
07
Ship, and remember it
Months later, the identical bug showed up on ShelemJ Resources. That time it took minutes, not hours, because the mechanism was already understood.
