Medicine and Science

The Medical Side I Don't Always Lead With

Medicine and Science · 7 min read

Most people who find this site land on the code first: the projects, the stack, the deployed sites. What tends to get less airtime is the four years before any of that, years spent in hospital laboratories and inside a state health insurance agency, doing work that had nothing to do with a browser. I want to write it down properly, not as a footnote to the developer story, but on its own terms.

What I actually did in the lab

At God Own Hospital in Oshodi, and before that as an intern at Paelon Memorial Hospital on Victoria Island, my days were built around biochemical assays, hematological evaluations, and microbiological cultures. That covers a wide range of concrete tasks: running spectrophotometry on blood chemistry panels, prepping and reading cultures for infection markers, calibrating and troubleshooting the equipment those results depend on. I also drew blood directly, phlebotomy was a routine part of the job, alongside specimen handling and processing from collection through to result.

None of that work tolerates guessing. A misread result, a contaminated sample, or a mislabeled tube isn't a cosmetic bug, it's a real risk to a real patient. I worked under strict SOPs, standard operating procedures, and quality control measures because that structure is what keeps a lab trustworthy, and I carried that structure with me everywhere after.

A different kind of care work, at LASHMA

My NYSC year looked completely different, and taught me something the lab couldn't. As a Provider Service Officer at Rauf Aregbesola Health Center under the Lagos State Health Insurance Agency, I sat at the point where healthcare policy meets an actual person trying to get covered for care. I guided patients through enrollment and eligibility, reviewed claims for compliance, and served as the liaison between healthcare providers and the agency when something didn't line up.

A lot of that work was quietly emotional. People came in confused, sometimes frustrated, sometimes worried about a family member's treatment. Explaining a benefits process clearly, and resolving a claims discrepancy without making someone feel like a number, turned out to matter as much as any technical skill I'd built in the lab. Empathy under pressure is its own competency, and I don't think I fully appreciated that until I was doing it daily.

Why I'm not leaving this chapter behind

I moved into frontend development because I wanted to build things people could use directly, and I've genuinely loved that shift. But I am not trying to erase four years of medical biochemistry, microbiology, and health insurance operations from my story to fit a single "tech developer" label. Both halves are real, both are current, and either one could be the reason someone reaches out to me next: a dev team that needs someone who ships carefully, or a lab, clinic, or health operations team that needs someone who already knows the discipline that world runs on.

If you're reading this from that second world, I see you, and I'd genuinely like to hear from you too. Back to blog Let's talk
